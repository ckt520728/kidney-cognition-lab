Unraveling Nested Oscillations, Nonlinear Amplitude Modulation, and Brain–Body Interactions Across Brain, Heart, and Metabolism

Welcome, colleagues. Today I am presenting our work on Holo-Hilbert Spectral Analysis, or HHSA, as a unifying mathematical framework for body signals across the brain, cardiovascular system, and human metabolism. Traditional spectral analysis assumes stationarity and treats biological rhythms as independent sums of sinusoids. But human physiology is intrinsically non-linear and non-stationary: slower physiological rhythms systematically modulate the amplitudes of faster carrier oscillations. Over the next twenty slides, following the SOIL framework, we will explore why traditional one-dimensional spectra fall short, how two-layer decomposition constructs a continuous three-dimensional energy cube, and how this reveals nested dynamics across continuous glucose monitoring, wearable heart rate variability, and neurodegenerative disease.

What a 1-D Spectrum Throws Away

Fourier and classical spectra mischaracterize amplitude modulation as spurious sidebands

03 / 25
The Sideband Fallacy

When an amplitude-modulated carrier wave is analyzed with Fourier or stationary wavelets, trigonometry dictates artificial sidebands:

x(t) = [1 + m · cos(ωAM t)] · cos(ωc t)
= cos(ωc t) + ½m · cos((ωc - ωAM)t) + ½m · cos((ωc + ωAM)t)

Physical reality is one carrier and one modulator. Fourier mathematics yields three uncoupled stationary sinusoids, scattering real physical power.

The Ambiguity of 1-D Power

A conventional 1-D power spectral density (PSD) or spectrogram S(f) collapses cross-frequency interactions. It cannot answer:

  • Are peaks at f1 and f2 two independent, uncoupled pacemakers?
  • Or is f1 a carrier whose amplitude is dynamically paced by f2?
Figure F1: 1-D collapsing vs. 3-D Holo-Hilbert expansion (t, f_carrier, f_AM).
What happens when you analyze an amplitude-modulated biological signal with a classical Fourier transform or static power spectrum? Fourier analysis projects the signal onto infinite, stationary sinusoids with constant amplitudes and phases. When a carrier wave undergoes amplitude modulation, Fourier mathematics cannot represent the varying amplitude directly. Instead, it generates spurious sideband frequencies on both sides of the carrier, scattering the true physical energy into mathematical artifacts. Furthermore, a one-dimensional power spectrum completely collapses cross-frequency interactions. You cannot tell whether two spectral peaks represent two independent, uncoupled physiological pacemakers or a single carrier rhythm whose strength is being modulated by a slower physiological cycle.

To capture dynamic interactions, signal processing has produced several important tools, summarized in Table One of our review. Phase-Amplitude Coupling, or PAC, identifies coupling between the phase of a slow band and the amplitude of a fast band. Opportunity (O) §2 The HHSA Framework Algorithmic Design

HHSA in One Picture: Decompose Twice

Two nested layers of empirical sifting resolve carrier frequency and modulation frequency

05 / 25
1️⃣ Layer 1: Carrier Decomposition

Decompose raw signal x(t) via noise-assisted EEMD or CEEMDAN into Intrinsic Mode Functions (IMFs):

x(t) = ∑i=1..M ci(t) + r(t)

Apply Hilbert Transform to each IMF ci(t) → extract Instantaneous Amplitude ai(t) and Instantaneous Carrier Frequency fc,i(t).

2️⃣ Layer 2: Modulation Decomposition

Treat each amplitude envelope ai(t) as an independent physical signal and sift again via EMD:

ai(t) = ∑j=1..P dij(t) + ri(t)

Apply Hilbert Transform to dij(t) → extract Instantaneous Modulation Frequency fAM,ij(t) and secondary amplitude Aij(t).

Figure F2: Two-layer sifting pipeline mapping raw signal to the 3-D Holo-Hilbert continuum.
The core insight of Holo-Hilbert Spectral Analysis, introduced by Norden Huang in 2016, is that resolving amplitude modulation requires decomposing the signal twice. In the first layer, the raw physiological signal undergoes noise-assisted Empirical Mode Decomposition, such as EEMD or CEEMDAN, yielding a finite set of Intrinsic Mode Functions. Each IMF admits a well-behaved instantaneous frequency and instantaneous amplitude via the Hilbert transform. In the second layer, rather than discarding the amplitude envelopes, HHSA treats each envelope as an independent physical time series and decomposes it again with empirical mode decomposition. This extracts secondary modulation IMFs, revealing the exact instantaneous frequencies at which the carrier amplitudes fluctuate over time.
Opportunity (O) §2 3-D Tensor Geometry Spectral Formulation

The Cube H(t, fc, fAM) & Its Three Projections

Orthogonal marginals recover Hilbert-Huang, cross-frequency, and modulation history maps

06 / 25
Projection 1 S(t, fc)

Hilbert-Huang Spectrum (HHT): Integrate H over modulation frequency fAM:

S(t, fc) = ∫ H(t, fc, fAM) dfAM

Classic time-frequency representation. Tracks instantaneous carrier power evolution over time.

Projection 2 C(fc, fAM)

Carrier vs. Modulation Spectrum: Integrate H over the entire time duration t:

C(fc, fAM) = ∫ H(t, fc, fAM) dt

Comprehensive cross-frequency coupling map. Identifies persistent nested rhythm coordinates.

Projection 3 M(t, fAM)

Modulation Time History: Integrate H over all carrier frequencies fc:

M(t, fAM) = ∫ H(t, fc, fAM) dfc

Dynamic modulation timeline. Reveals when physiological AM bursts wax and wane.

Mathematical Consistency: Exact numerical validation confirms that summing our discrete 3-D tensors along each orthogonal axis reproduces K2 projections with relative error < 10-6 (verified in cube_validation.json).

By executing this two-layer decomposition, HHSA expands signal representation from a one-dimensional spectrum into a continuous three-dimensional energy density tensor, H of t, f carrier, and f AM. To make this tensor clinically interpretable, we define three orthogonal marginal projections. Integrating across the modulation axis yields S of t and f carrier, which is the classical Hilbert-Huang time-frequency spectrum. Integrating over time yields C of f carrier and f AM, the carrier-versus-modulation spectrum that maps cross-frequency coupling across the entire recording. Integrating across the carrier axis yields M of t and f AM, illustrating how modulation intensity waxes and wanes over physiological time.
Opportunity (O) §2, §6 Physical Validity Rigorous Bounds

Admissibility (fAM < fc) & Duration Bounds (f ≥ 3/T)

Distinguishing true physiological amplitude modulation from mathematical artifacts

07 / 25
⚖️ 1. The Admissibility Condition
fAM < fc   (Strict Physical Inequality)

Physical Principle: An envelope cannot oscillate faster than the carrier wave it modulates (Bedrosian Theorem on product spectra).

  • Any coordinate where fAM ≥ fc represents cubic spline overshoot, mode mixing, or noise leakage.
  • Our 3-D explorer and pipeline construct an explicit admissibility mask to exclude unphysical energy.
⏱️ 2. The 3-Cycle Duration Bound
fresolvable ≥ 3 / T   (T = Recording Duration)

Physical Principle: Adaptive sifting requires at least 3 local extrema (troughs and peaks) to interpolate upper and lower envelopes reliably.

  • 13-day CGM (T = 312 h): f ≥ 3/312 ≈ 0.0096 c/h. Circadian (0.042 c/h) is fully resolvable.
  • 7-min Wearable HRV (T = 420 s): f ≥ 3/420 ≈ 0.0071 Hz. VLF (< 0.007 Hz) is mathematically unresolvable and grey-hatched!
Not every mathematical point in the Holo-Hilbert volume is physically meaningful. Two rigorous constraints must be enforced. First is the mathematical admissibility condition: an envelope cannot oscillate faster than the carrier wave it modulates. Coordinates where f AM is greater than or equal to f carrier violate the Bedrosian theorem and represent spline overshoot or mode-mixing artifacts; these must be masked out. Second is the physiological record-length bound: resolving an oscillatory mode reliably via empirical mode decomposition requires at least three complete cycles, meaning frequency must exceed three divided by duration T. For a seven-minute wearable recording, frequencies below point zero zero seven Hertz are unresolvable and must be greyed out.

Leverage (L) §6 Methodological Integrity Pitfall Ledger

Five Pitfalls That Change the Answer

Common technical errors that generate artificial amplitude modulation and false positives

16 / 25
1. Nyquist & Aliasing

Under-sampling folds high-frequency noise directly into physiological bands.

Rule: Sensor sampling rate fs must be strictly > 2 × max carrier frequency, with analog anti-aliasing filtering.

2. Insufficient Duration T

Attempting to extract rhythms slower than 3 / T causes severe envelope smearing.

Rule: Strictly mask or grey-out bins below 3 / T in all published spectra.

3. Mode Mixing

Intermittent signals cause single IMFs to jump across multiple frequency octaves.

Rule: Always use noise-assisted sifting (EEMD or CEEMDAN) with ≥ 100 ensemble trials.

4. Edge Spline Distortion

Cubic spline interpolation diverges at signal boundaries, distorting Hilbert phases.

Rule: Systematically trim 5% to 10% of samples from both ends prior to spectral evaluation.

5. Spurious AM (fAM ≥ fc)

Allowing unmasked coordinates where modulation is faster than carrier violates Bedrosian theorem.

Rule: Enforce the mathematical admissibility plane mask fAM < fc.

The Antidote: Null Testing

Always benchmark observed peaks against phase-randomized (IAAFT) surrogate time series.

Verifies whether AM energy exceeds stochastic linear noise baselines.

Applying Holo-Hilbert analysis to biological time series without rigorous methodological safeguards produces misleading conclusions. We highlight five critical pitfalls. First, violating the Nyquist criterion or inadequate sampling rates creates severe aliasing. Second, analyzing records shorter than three cycles of the target rhythm produces unresolvable low-frequency spectral smearing. Third, single-sift empirical mode decomposition suffers from mode mixing, necessitating noise-assisted ensemble methods. Fourth, spline fitting at the beginning and end of finite signals introduces severe boundary distortion, requiring systematic edge trimming of five to ten percent. Finally, failing to mask coordinates where modulation frequency exceeds carrier frequency generates spurious, non-physical amplitude modulation.

To elevate the reproducibility and rigor of physiological HHSA, Table Three of our review proposes an eight-point reporting standard. Every future study should report: first, the exact decomposition algorithm and ensemble parameters; second, the instantaneous frequency calculation method; third, explicit enforcement of the mathematical admissibility mask; fourth, the minimum resolvable frequency based on three cycles divided by record duration; fifth, the exact boundary trimming percentage; sixth, surrogate data null testing against phase-randomized controls; seventh, transparent ethical provenance and de-identification procedures; and eighth, open-source code availability. Adhering to these eight criteria ensures that published amplitude modulation findings represent authentic physiological coupling rather than numerical artifacts.
